Project 01 | Quantitative finance

Quantitative Portfolio Research Pipeline

This project tests how a diversified ETF allocation balances return and risk against SPY. It turns historical market data into portfolio weights, evaluates those choices on later returns, and makes the assumptions, calculations, and transaction costs available for review.

Why this matters | Decision support and trust

Investment decisions need evidence people can understand and check.

Clients and stakeholders entrust investment teams with substantial capital, sometimes millions or billions. When a portfolio gains or loses value, they want to understand the decisions behind it, the risks taken, and whether the outcome is consistent with the investment approach. Unexplained results can weaken trust even when performance is positive.

This pipeline makes the research traceable: historical prices feed documented financial calculations, allocation constraints make the investment rules explicit, and walk-forward testing evaluates weights on subsequent returns with modeled trading costs. Reviewers can inspect the allocation history, compare return and drawdown against SPY, and rerun the analysis under different assumptions.

For a quantitative developer, the value is building a reliable connection between an investment idea and evidence that portfolio managers can examine and explain. This project supports that investigation and makes its assumptions visible; measuring each asset's exact contribution to gains and losses would require a dedicated performance-attribution analysis.

Research question

Can a constrained ETF portfolio improve risk-adjusted performance?

The experiment tests a six-ETF portfolio covering Canadian equities, US equities, developed international equities, emerging markets, aggregate bonds, and gold.

The strategy maximizes expected Sharpe ratio subject to long-only weights, full investment, and a 30% cap per asset. It is compared with SPY using only information available before each rebalance date, with transaction costs charged whenever allocations change.

The result is intentionally not presented as a trading product. It is a research workflow: define assumptions, load data, calculate risk and return, optimize under constraints, backtest out of sample, and validate the calculation path.

A configuration change reruns the complete experiment and regenerates every output.

Pandas and NumPy calculate returns, covariance, volatility, drawdown, Sharpe, and VaR.

03

Optimizer and backtest

Weights are solved under constraints, frozen for the next quarter, and charged transaction costs.

04

Validation layer

MATLAB cross-check and 44 pytest cases protect the calculations and chronology.

Research finding

From research idea to reproducible system

SPY produced the higher raw return in this sample. The optimized portfolio produced lower volatility, a smaller maximum drawdown, and a higher Sharpe ratio after modeled transaction costs. The report presents that trade-off directly instead of claiming an unrealistic win. It also beat every rule that estimates nothing, which is the comparison that tests whether the optimization was worth doing.

The important point is not that the model wins every comparison. The important point is that the assumptions, calculations, constraints, costs, and validation checks are visible enough to review.

Engineering controls

  • Python and pandas return/risk pipeline
  • SQL CTEs, joins, aggregations, and window functions
  • Independent MATLAB implementation cross-check
  • Pytest validation for returns, constraints, drawdowns, costs, and chronology
  • No look-ahead in the rebalance loop
  • Configuration-driven assumptions for lookback, rebalance frequency, weight caps, costs, and benchmark
  • Ledoit-Wolf covariance shrinkage and James-Stein shrinkage of expected returns
  • Naive baselines run on identical rebalance dates, so comparisons are paired

Naive baseline comparison

Did optimizing beat not optimizing?

Comparing an optimizer with SPY asks whether the asset mix was better. Comparing it with equal weight asks whether the optimization earned its keep. Every rule below is run on identical rebalance dates and identical training windows, so a difference between two of them comes from the rule rather than from one having traded on luckier days.

Optimizing helped here — and that is not a general law. Maximum Sharpe reached 0.79 against 0.63 for equal weight, the best rule that estimates nothing. Six ETFs spanning bonds, gold and four equity regions have genuinely different risk profiles, so the covariance carries structure worth exploiting. On a more correlated single-country stock universe the same class of optimizer loses to equal weight — which is the DeMiguel, Garlappi and Uppal (2009) result, and which my other project reproduces.

Robustness

Does the conclusion survive its assumptions?

A backtest reports one number per configuration, and somebody chose the configuration. A 36-month lookback, a 30% cap and 10 basis points are all defensible and all arbitrary. Varying each one at a time, maximum Sharpe ranks first in 9 of the 11 configurations tried and minimum variance in the other 2, so the finding is not an artifact of one setting.

One caveat is built into the output rather than left for the reader to spot: a longer lookback needs more history before the first rebalance, so it also starts the evaluation later and scores a shorter period. That raises every strategy's Sharpe ratio including the benchmark's. Rows are comparable within themselves; levels across rows are not, and the benchmark column is there to make the shift visible.

Sensitivity grid · Sensitivity ranking

Independent MATLAB implementation. Minimum variance and risk parity are re-solved in MATLAB with no shared solver — accelerated projected gradient and closed-form coordinate descent, written out rather than delegated to a toolbox — and the script asserts agreement with the Python answer rather than printing numbers for a reader to compare. It runs in GNU Octave, so a reviewer without a MATLAB licence can check the result, and continuous integration runs it on every push.

Setting this up properly surfaced a real defect. The exported covariance was the full-sample matrix while the published weights were fitted to the latest 36-month window, so the cross-check had been solving a different problem from the one it claimed to verify. The comparison now uses the matrix the weights were actually solved from, and agrees to 6e-7.
